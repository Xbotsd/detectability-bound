# detectability-bound

[![DOI](https://zenodo.org/badge/DOI/10.5281/zenodo.24080212.svg)](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24080212)

**How big does a flaw have to be before an inspection can see it — and how much of what you measure is the flaw versus the measurement?**

This repository contains the derivation, the reference implementation and the
real-data test of a closed-form upper bound on the probability of detection (POD)
of a volumetric flaw. No new imaging is required to use it: the bound is computed
from three numbers that are known before any coupon is made.

```
d'(a) = (ΔI / σ) · √E(a/L) · √N
POD(a) ≤ Φ( d'(a) − z_{1−α} )
```

`E(a/L)` is the squared norm of the flaw image after blur, `L` the point-spread
width, `N` the number of independent looks. The bound follows from the
Neyman–Pearson lemma and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality: the matched filter
maximises the deflection, so no detector — including any trained one — can exceed
it.

## The two asymptotes

| regime | deflection | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `a ≪ L` | `d' ∝ (a/L)³` | below the voxel size, a flaw is a volume: halving it costs a factor of eight |
| `a ≫ L` | `d' ∝ (a/L)^{3/2}` | above it, the ordinary energy-versus-noise law |

Corollary: **one halving of the voxel size is worth 64 repeat scans** below the
voxel size, and 8 above it.

## The design rule

Largest admissible voxel size for POD ≥ 0.90 at α = 0.01, single look:

| flaw a (µm) | ΔI/σ = 2 | 5 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | 20.5 | 33.1 | 46.2 |
| 100 | 41.0 | 66.1 | 92.3 |
| 200 | 82.1 | 132.2 | 184.6 |
| 500 | 205.2 | 330.6 | 461.6 |

The admissible voxel scales nearly proportionally with the flaw and only weakly
with contrast: **money is better spent on resolution than on dose.**

## The part the simulations cannot tell you

On a public X-ray CT stack of laser powder-bed AlSi10Mg (212 slices, 37 µm
voxels) the geometry transfers intact but the level does not. The noise a matched
filter actually sees is **κ = 24 to 125 times the voxel noise**, because the
statistic integrates microstructure rather than voxels:

| x = a/L | 0.70 | 1.40 | 2.79 | 4.19 | 5.59 | 8.38 | 11.17 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| κ | 31.2 | 24.4 | 25.9 | 38.0 | 51.0 | 87.8 | 125.2 |

κ is flat below the voxel size, so the cubic law survives with its amplitude
divided by twenty-five; above it κ grows almost as fast as √E, so for flaws of
equal contrast the effective exponent drops from 3/2 to **about 0.3**. The bound
is never violated — it is slack, by a factor that is measurable and that is
reported rather than absorbed.

## What is and is not synthetic

Everything empirical — σ, the PSF width, κ, the measured POD — comes from the
public XCT record cited in `data/README.md`; the raw slices are not committed, but
every slice used is listed with its SHA-256 so the exact data set can be
re-fetched and verified.

The one injected quantity is the flaw: known spheres are *placed into real metal*,
so the background, the noise correlation and the artefacts are the scanner's and
only the defect is planted. That is stated in the paper and in the code.

`python -m dbound verify` runs a computation with exact truth. It checks the
algebra of Propositions 1 and 2 and is never presented as an experiment.
